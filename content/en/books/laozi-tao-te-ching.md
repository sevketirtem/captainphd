## In brief
The *Tao Te Ching* is a short Chinese text traditionally attributed to Laozi. It is about the *dao*, "the way", and how a person, and a ruler, can live in accord with it. A recurring theme is that more is achieved by letting things take their course than by forcing them.

## About the book
The text is divided into 81 short chapters, often in poetic and enigmatic form. It uses images such as water, which is soft yet can wear down stone, and the emptiness that makes a pot useful. A central concept is *wu wei*, often translated as "effortless action" or "not acting against the course of nature". Who wrote the text, and when, is disputed among scholars. The Project Gutenberg edition is James Legge's 1891 translation, among the earliest in English. Translations of this book vary greatly, so read several if you can.

## From the bookshelf to daily life
This is our own reading, not Laozi's.

**Programming.**
- The idea of not over-controlling can be read as advice on simplicity. A program with fewer parts, fewer dependencies and fewer special cases is often easier to maintain.
- The emptiness that makes the pot useful can recall interfaces: what is left out is sometimes what makes a system usable.

**Seafaring.**
- Seafarers know that you rarely win against the sea by using more force. Good seamanship uses current, wind and tide rather than fighting them.
- Rule 8(b) asks that alterations of course be large enough to be readily apparent to other ships. That is the opposite of hesitant small adjustments, and shows that clear, calm action is often safest.

## Objections
- The text is enigmatic and permits many readings. It is easy to read in what you already wish to find.
- Letting things take their course is poor advice when something demands action, such as in an emergency.
- The origin is uncertain, and Legge's interpretation dates from the nineteenth century. Newer scholarship may see it differently.
