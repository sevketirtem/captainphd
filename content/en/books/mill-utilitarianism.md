## In brief
*Utilitarianism* is Mill's short defence of the idea that actions are right in so far as they promote happiness and wrong in so far as they produce the opposite. What counts is happiness for everyone affected, not only for the one who acts. The book appeared in 1861 and is one of the most widely read introductions to moral philosophy.

## About the book
Mill answers common objections, among them that the doctrine makes people into swine chasing pleasure. He holds that not all pleasures are worth the same: those who use their abilities fully tend to rank intellectual and moral pleasures higher. He also discusses why we should care about others' happiness, how justice relates to utility, and why rules learned from experience are useful guides. His ideas build on Jeremy Bentham, but Mill is more nuanced about what counts as happiness.

## From the bookshelf to daily life
This is our own reading, not Mill's.

**Programming.**
- Utilitarianism resembles optimisation: we choose what gives the best overall result. But what you measure decides what you get. Measure clicks and you get clicks, not necessarily anything useful.
- Mill's point that rules rest on experience resembles good coding practice: we follow guidelines because they usually give better results, not because they are sacred.

**Seafaring.**
- Rule 6 on safe speed asks the master to weigh several factors, among them visibility, traffic density and manoeuvrability. Choosing a speed that balances progress and safety for everyone nearby is a practical question of weighing.
- When a ship must choose between two bad outcomes, we implicitly ask what harms fewest. Mill's way of thinking shows both the strength and the danger of calculating with human lives.

## Objections
- Happiness is hard to measure and compare between people.
- In some cases the doctrine can justify sacrificing an individual for the majority. Many think this violates basic rights.
- The distinction between "higher" and "lower" pleasures can seem pompous or elitist.
