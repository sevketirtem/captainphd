## In brief
*Politics* is Aristotle's work on how a city (*polis*) should be governed and why people live together. He holds that humans are social beings and that a city exists to make a good life possible, not merely survival. Much of the book compares forms of government and asks what keeps a state stable.

## About the book
Aristotle starts from the bottom: the household, then the village, then the city. He distinguishes rule for the common good from rule for the benefit of the rulers, and sets up a scheme of three good forms and three corrupt ones. He also discusses why states fall into revolt and what might prevent it. One of his best-known points is that a large, solid middle class often makes a state more stable than a sharp split into rich and poor. The Project Gutenberg edition is an older English translation by William Ellis; the language is dated.

## From the bookshelf to daily life
This is our own reading, not Aristotle's.

**Programming.**
- A team is a small *polis*. Who decides, how disagreement is resolved and who carries responsibility determine whether the work lasts, just as the form of government does for a city.
- Aristotle's interest in what makes institutions stable resembles work on systems: what happens when one group gets too much power, and how do you stop the rules from serving only those who write them?

**Seafaring.**
- A ship is a small society with different tasks and one shared aim: to arrive safely. No one on board can do everything, and everyone needs the others.
- COLREG Rule 5 on look-out shows that safety is a shared duty, not the master's alone. Rule 2(a) places responsibility on the ship, owner, master and crew together.

## Objections
- Aristotle defends slavery and holds women to be subordinate to men. We cannot read past this, and it weakens parts of his argument.
- His picture of the city is drawn from small Greek city-states, which differ greatly from modern states.
- The Gutenberg translation is old. Read a newer one if you want easier language.
