## In brief
The *Republic* is Plato's longest and best-known dialogue. Socrates and some friends ask what justice is and whether the just person lives better than the unjust one. To answer, they sketch an ideal city and, alongside it, a model of the soul. Along the way come three famous images: the cave, the line and the ship.

## About the book
The book has ten books. In Book I Socrates and Thrasymachus try to define justice, and Thrasymachus claims that justice is only the interest of the stronger. Glaucon and Adeimantus challenge Socrates to show that justice is worth something in itself. Socrates answers with an ideal city of three groups (rulers, guardians and craftsmen) and with a parallel inner order of the soul in reason, spirit and appetite. Justice is each part doing its own work. Books VI and VII bring the images: the one who knows the good is like someone who has seen the sun, and in the allegory of the cave in Book VII we are told how most people see shadows and take them for reality. Books VIII and IX describe how cities and persons decay, and Book X ends with art and the myth of Er. Jowett's introduction in the edition offers a reading guide.

## The ship in Book VI
Socrates asks us to imagine a ship. The captain is large and strong, but hard of hearing and poor at navigation. The crew quarrel over who should steer, though none has learned the craft. They press the captain to hand them the helm, and those who fail to win are overruled or thrown overboard. The one who actually understands the winds, the seasons, the sky and the stars is called by the crew a fool, a prater and a star-gazer. Plato's point is that government is an art that has to be learned, and that those who know it should not have to beg to use it.

## From the bookshelf to daily life
This is our own reading, not Plato's.

**Seafaring.**
- The image is nearly a caricature of a bridge without clear roles. A modern reader quickly sees an argument for competence and clear responsibility. The ISM Code gives the master overriding authority (section 5.2), but that is not the same as the master not listening. Plato's captain is weak precisely because he does not know the craft.
- Rule 8 (a) of the collision regulations says manoeuvres shall be made "in accordance with good seamanship". Plato's point is that good seamanship is knowledge, not opinion.

**Programming.**
- Technical competence and voting fit badly together. A team voting on an architecture when nobody knows the subject ends up as the crew in the parable. That does not mean everyone should defer to whoever shouts loudest, but that decisions should rest on what those who know the matter actually say.
- Conversely there is a risk in "those who know" staying away: Plato himself says the wise should not beg for power, and that can become an excuse for not getting involved.

## Objections
- Plato argues for a strict hierarchy, censorship of art, and the rulers holding property and children in common. Many readers find the ideal city disturbing, and some call it totalitarian.
- The ship image is an argument against democracy, and democracy's defenders reply that expertise is not the same as legitimacy. A captain has power over a ship, but a state has citizens who did not sign on.
- The justice of the book depends on the soul and the city each having three parts. That is an assumption, not a proof.
