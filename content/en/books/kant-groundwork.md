## In brief
Kant wanted to find the basic principle of morality, independent of what happens to benefit us. His answer is the categorical imperative: act only on rules you could will to be rules for everyone. The book appeared in 1785 and is short but hard to read.

## About the book
Kant begins with the claim that the only thing good without qualification is a good will. Actions have moral worth when done from duty, not merely because they suit us. He then gives several formulations of the categorical imperative. One is the universal-law test: could your rule be a rule for everyone? Another says we must always treat persons, ourselves and others, also as ends and never merely as means. The Project Gutenberg edition is Abbott's nineteenth-century translation.

## From the bookshelf to daily life
This is our own reading, not Kant's.

**Programming.**
- The universal-law test is like a code review: what happens if everyone does what I am doing now? If everyone skipped tests, nobody could trust anything.
- Treating users as ends and not merely as means is a good question for every product: do we serve the user, or only exploit their attention?

**Seafaring.**
- The collision regulations work because everyone follows the same rules. A ship that keeps the rules only when it suits it turns everyone else into pieces in its own game.
- Rule 2(a) stresses that the rules do not exonerate anyone from the consequences of neglecting precautions required by good seamanship. This resembles Kant's idea that duty is more than following the letter.

## Objections
- Kant offers little help when duties collide. What do you do when two rules point in different directions?
- Focusing on rules and intention rather than consequences can give harsh results in concrete cases.
- The language is dense and the translation old. Many readers use a commentary alongside.
