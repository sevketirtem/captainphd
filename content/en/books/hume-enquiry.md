## In brief
*An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* is David Hume's short account of what we can know and how we come to know it. The best-known question is this: we assume the future will resemble the past, but what is the ground for believing it? Hume holds that there is no strict proof of it. We do it out of habit.

## About the book
Hume divides what we can know into two: relations of ideas (as in mathematics, where the opposite would be a contradiction) and matters of fact (what actually happens in the world). Of matters of fact he says that the claim "the sun will not rise tomorrow" is no less intelligible than that it will rise, and contains no contradiction. What we rely on to say anything about the unobserved is experience of things having followed one another. Hume calls the principle behind this custom or habit. He holds that we cannot explain it further, and that it is a basic feature of human nature. The book also treats free will, miracles and the limits of what reason can establish. The Project Gutenberg edition is edited by L. A. Selby-Bigge.

## From the bookshelf to daily life
This is our own reading, not Hume's.

**Programming.**
- That all tests are green today does not prove the program will work tomorrow. Tests say that things went well under conditions we checked. That resembles Hume's point: past experience applies only as far as the future resembles it.
- "It works on my machine" is a claim about what we have seen. When the environment changes (new version, different load, different user), it is no longer a given.

**Seafaring.**
- Radar plotting rests on Hume's problem: we draw up a target from the course and speed it has had, and assume it will continue. Rule 7 (c) says conclusions must not be drawn on the basis of scanty information, especially scanty radar information. That is a practical safety rule against relying too much on habit.
- Rule 7 (d) (i) says risk of collision shall be deemed to exist if the compass bearing of an approaching vessel does not appreciably change, and (ii) that such risk may sometimes exist even when the bearing does change, for example when approaching a very large vessel. The Rules use experience as a signal but build in a safety margin because experience can fail.
- Rule 7 (a) says that if there is any doubt, such risk shall be deemed to exist. When knowledge is uncertain, the safest thing is to assume the worst.

## Objections
- Immanuel Kant wrote that Hume woke him from his "dogmatic slumber", and tried himself to give the concept of cause a different foundation.
- Many hold that we can safely trust experience in practice even if we cannot prove it: nobody lives as if the sun might not rise. Hume himself says we must live by habit.
- Hume is sceptical about miracles and religion, which has been controversial then and now.
