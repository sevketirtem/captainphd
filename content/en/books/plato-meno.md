## In brief
*Meno* is a short dialogue in which Meno asks Socrates whether virtue can be taught. Socrates answers that he does not even know what virtue is, and the conversation moves, via a geometry experiment with a slave boy, to a distinction between knowledge and true opinion. It is one of the easiest ways into Plato.

## About the book
Meno proposes several answers about what virtue is, and Socrates shows they are only examples, not a definition. Meno replies with a paradox: how can one search for something one does not know, and how would one recognise it? Socrates answers with a doctrine that the soul has seen everything before, and has one of Meno's slaves, an untrained boy, work out how to double a square. The boy goes wrong at first, realises he does not know, and then finds the answer with the help of Socrates' questions. Then they discuss whether virtue is knowledge, and why able men fail to teach virtue to their sons. Socrates concludes that true opinion can guide as well as knowledge, but is unstable: it may "run away" like the statues of Daedalus unless tied down with an account of why.

## From the bookshelf to daily life
This is our own reading, not Plato's.

**Programming.**
- The distinction between true opinion and knowledge is the distinction between code that happens to work and code you understand. If you copy a solution from the internet and it fits, you have a true opinion. When the environment changes, it "runs away" because you do not know why it worked.
- Socrates' questions to the boy's first wrong answer are nearly the same as using a debugger: what did you expect, what happened, what does it tell you?

**Seafaring.**
- A mate who follows an ECDIS route without understanding why it lies where it does has a true opinion as long as everything matches. Rule 7 (c) says conclusions must not be drawn on scanty information. Knowledge means knowing what must be true, not only what the instrument says.
- The book is also a reminder of how much is learned by being asked. We wrote about this in the review of Rancière, who uses a similar idea.

## Objections
- The doctrine that the soul remembers what it has seen before is not accepted today, and the boy's geometry shows more how much a leading line of questions can do than that the knowledge was innate.
- The dialogue ends without a final definition of virtue. Many readers find that frustrating.
- Socrates treats the boy as a piece of property to be used for the demonstration, in a culture where slavery was normal.
