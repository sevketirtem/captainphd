## In brief
*On Liberty* (1859) by John Stuart Mill is about how far society has the right to interfere in the individual's life. Mill's answer is a simple principle: the only legitimate purpose for restricting a person's freedom against his will is to prevent harm to others. His own good, physical or moral, is not enough. The book is best known for its defence of the freedom to think, speak and discuss.

## About the book
The book has five chapters: introductory, liberty of thought and discussion, individuality, the limits to the authority of society over the individual, and applications. In chapter II Mill argues that a suppressed opinion may be true, that it may contain part of the truth, and that even a true opinion becomes a dead dogma if it is never challenged. Chapter III says it is valuable that people live differently and try out different ways of life. Mill is also concerned about pressure that comes not only from the law but from public opinion, "the tyranny of the majority". The Project Gutenberg edition also has an introduction.

## From the bookshelf to daily life
This is our own reading, not Mill's.

**Programming.**
- Code review and open source are in practice Mill's chapter II: a change nobody is allowed to criticise is a promise without a check. An experienced developer who is questioned by a beginner may discover that she no longer knows why something was done that way.
- "Dead dogma" is familiar in teams: rules and habits followed because they have always been there, without anyone knowing what they protect against.

**Seafaring.**
- The harm principle fits well with the collision regulations. Rule 2 says that nobody is exonerated from the consequences of neglecting precautions, and the whole regime limits vessels' freedom in order to prevent harm to others. At the same time Rule 2 (b) allows departure from the Rules when necessary to avoid immediate danger. Freedom and responsibility are tied together here.
- Mill is an argument for a bridge where everyone can speak up. Safety culture at sea rests on the junior officer being able to question the master's plan, and on this being received as help.
- The collision regulations are an example of freedoms that should be limited because the consequences fall on others, but the limits should have a clear purpose.

## Objections
- "Harm to others" is not as simple as it sounds. What counts as harm, and what about indirect or psychological harm?
- Mill also writes that despotism can be a legitimate mode of government in dealing with "barbarians", if the end is their improvement. That attitude has been used to defend colonialism, and it is hard to read today.
- Critics have pointed out that free speech in practice depends on who holds power and a platform, which Mill only partly addresses.
