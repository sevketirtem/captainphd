## In brief
Marcus Aurelius was Roman emperor from 161 to 180. *Meditations* is twelve books of short notes that he wrote in Greek, first and foremost for himself. Most scholars think they were not meant for publication. The tone is therefore different from a textbook: this is a man reminding himself of what he knows but does not always do. The text lies close to the Stoics, such as Epictetus, whom we review separately.

## About the book
The notes do not form a continuous argument, and the order is not chronological. A few themes recur:
- **Impermanence.** Everything, fame and power included, passes quickly. Marcus does not use this to become sad but to keep things in proportion.
- **Judgements.** Much of what troubles us lies in what we think about a matter, not in the matter itself.
- **Duty and community.** We are made to work together, and a deed has worth when it serves others.
- **Understanding others.** People act on what they believe to be good. That leaves less to be astonished about.
- **Starting the day.** Several notes remind him to get up and do his work, even when it is hard.

The Project Gutenberg edition uses Meric Casaubon's translation of 1634. The language is old (thee, thine), and anyone wanting something lighter can find newer editions, though they are not necessarily in the public domain.

## From the bookshelf to daily life
This is our own reading, not Marcus Aurelius'.

**Programming.**
- Keeping a log of what one did and thought, as Marcus did, resembles an engineering log or a short decision diary. You can spot patterns in your own mistakes.
- The idea that people act on their own beliefs is useful in code review: whoever wrote the odd thing had a reason, even if it was not a good one.

**Seafaring.**
- In Book VIII (in Casaubon's translation) it says that a physician should not wonder that a patient has a fever, nor a pilot that the wind is contrary. A seafarer knows the picture: the weather comes, and the job is to sail the ship accordingly.
- The notes were written by a man who led armies and made decisions under pressure. That makes them easier to use for those who keep a watch or carry responsibility than many purely philosophical texts.

## Objections
- The text is often repetitive and somewhat grim, and those who read it as a unified doctrine risk over-interpreting it.
- Marcus himself was the supreme ruler of an empire that waged war and practised slavery. The relation between his virtues and his power is a question many readers ask.
- Reading seventeenth-century translations can obscure the content. It is worth comparing with a newer edition.
