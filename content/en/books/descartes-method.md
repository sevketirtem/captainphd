*Review of a public-domain book. Written by Captain PhD, 9 November 2026.*

## In brief
*Discours de la méthode* is a short, autobiographical text in which René Descartes explains how he arrived at his way of thinking. It was originally the preface to three scientific treatises, and it is written in French rather than Latin so that more people could read it. The most famous line is "I think, therefore I am". The most useful part for us is the practical one: a small procedure for solving hard problems.

## About the book
The book has six parts. Part I covers Descartes' education and why he became dissatisfied with much of what he had learned. Part II sets out four rules of thinking: accept nothing as true until it is evident, divide a problem into as many pieces as needed, proceed from the simplest to the more complex, and make enumerations and reviews so complete that nothing is overlooked. Part III is a "provisional morality", a few rules of conduct he follows while doubting everything else. Part IV is the metaphysics, with the famous starting point that even if he doubts everything, he cannot doubt that he is doubting. Part V sketches a mechanical account of the body and the circulation of the blood. Part VI explains why he published what he did and why he thinks science should serve practical ends.

## From the bookshelf to daily life
This is our own reading, not Descartes'.

**Programming.**
- Rules two and three are, in practice, how we work on a bug or a system: split it up, and build from the simple parts. Halving the search for a fault (as `git bisect` does) is rule two in a machine.
- Rule four, the review, is code review and test coverage: have we checked all the cases, or only the ones we thought of?
- Doubting in order to find firm ground resembles writing a test that must fail first, so that we know it really measures something.

**Seafaring.**
- Descartes uses an image we recognise: a traveller lost in a forest should pick one direction and keep to it rather than wander back and forth. His point concerns choices when nothing clearly favours any option. Compare Rule 8 (b) of the collision regulations: a succession of small alterations should be avoided, and an alteration must be large enough to be apparent to others. The likeness is that hesitation confuses. The difference matters: the Rules require us to check that the manoeuvre is working (Rule 8 (d)), whereas Descartes' traveller gets no new information on the way.
- Rule 7 (c) says that assumptions must not be made on the basis of scanty information, especially scanty radar information. That is close in spirit to Descartes' first rule, accepting nothing that is not clear.

## Objections
- Descartes' reasoning about God and the world has been criticised since his own time, among other things for being circular.
- The mind–body split that the book lays the ground for has proved hard to explain: how can the two affect each other?
- The physiology in Part V is partly wrong by today's knowledge, and the idea of animals as machines is hard to defend now.
- Part VI speaks of becoming "masters and possessors of nature". After the climate crisis many readers find that phrase more uncomfortable than Descartes' contemporaries did.
