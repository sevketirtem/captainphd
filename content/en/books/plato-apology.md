## In brief
The *Apology* is Plato's account of the speech Socrates gave in court in Athens when he was accused of not honouring the city's gods and of corrupting the young. He was sentenced to death. The text is short and easy to read, and it is one of the clearest pictures we have of what it means to examine one's own and other people's assumptions.

## About the book
Socrates tells how his friend Chaerephon asked the oracle at Delphi whether anyone was wiser than Socrates, and the oracle said no. Socrates took it as a riddle and began to investigate. He went to politicians, poets and craftsmen. He found that they thought they knew much that they did not know. He himself knew no more, but he knew that he did not know, and that was the small difference. He says he is like a gadfly on a large, sluggish horse: the city needs someone to keep it awake. In the speech after the verdict he says that an unexamined life is not worth living. Near the end he speaks of death and says that no one knows it to be an evil. Jowett's introduction in the edition gives background on the trial.

## From the bookshelf to daily life
This is our own reading, not Plato's.

**Programming.**
- Socrates' method, asking simple questions about something that seems obvious, is the same as explaining a bug to a rubber duck or asking "why" five times in an incident review. Whoever has to explain his code often discovers what he has not understood.
- Knowing what you do not know is valuable in a field where everything changes fast. Whoever says "I haven't used this before" early saves the project a lot of time.

**Seafaring.**
- Rule 7 (c) says that conclusions must not be drawn on the basis of scanty information. That is a practical form of Socratic humility: we know less than we think, and must investigate before we act.
- A bridge where everyone dares to question the master and each other is safer than one where authority decides. That is the core of what is now called bridge resource management, even though Plato did not write about ships.

## Objections
- We do not know how accurately Plato reports the historical Socrates. Much suggests he made him his own voice.
- Socrates' method can be tiring and can end with nobody getting an answer. It does not always lead to a solution.
- The speech is the defence of one party. The accusers do not get to speak in this book.
