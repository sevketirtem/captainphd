## In brief
The *Nicomachean Ethics* is Aristotle's main work on how a person can live well. His answer is that a good life is a life in accordance with virtue (Greek *arete*, which can also be translated "excellence"). Virtues are not something we are born with but something we acquire through practice, and they must be used with judgement in each situation.

## About the book
The book has ten books. The first deal with the aim of human life, happiness or flourishing (Greek *eudaimonia*), which he places in using one's abilities well over a whole life. In Book II he says that virtues of character, such as courage and temperance, are formed by habit: we become just by doing just acts. Each virtue lies between two extremes, too little and too much. The mean is not the same for everyone: it must be "relative to us", not a mathematical average. Book VI treats practical wisdom (*phronesis*), the ability to see what is right to do in a particular case. Later books cover friendship and pleasure, and Book X closes with the place of contemplation in life. The Project Gutenberg edition has an introduction by J. A. Smith and a translation by D. P. Chase.

## From the bookshelf to daily life
This is our own reading, not Aristotle's.

**Programming.**
- Becoming a good programmer is an example of his idea: we learn the craft by writing a lot of code, having it read and fixing it. Books and courses help, but the habits are built in the work.
- The mean exists in the field. Too little testing is irresponsible, too much can slow everything down. Too little abstraction gives repetition, too much gives unreadable code. What is right depends on the project.

**Seafaring.**
- Rule 6 on safe speed is a good example of the "mean relative to us". The rule gives no number but a list of considerations: visibility, traffic density, manoeuvrability and more. The right speed for one ship in one situation is wrong for another. This is practical wisdom in a rule text.
- Rule 2 (a) refers to "the ordinary practice of seamen". That is a habit built up in a community over time, and is close to Aristotle's idea that virtue is practised judgement.
- Virtues such as courage and caution each have a fault on both sides. Courage without caution becomes recklessness, and caution without courage becomes paralysis. Both are dangerous on a bridge.

## Objections
- "The mean" can seem vague. How do you know the right amount? Aristotle answers: the judgement of the wise person decides, and that can look like a circular argument.
- Aristotle wrote in a society where slavery and the subordination of women were taken for granted, and the texts bear the mark of it.
- The book presupposes a certain upbringing. Someone who did not acquire good habits as a child, he holds, finds it harder to become good later.
