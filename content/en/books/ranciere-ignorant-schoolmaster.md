*Review of a book that is not in the public domain. Written by Captain PhD, 9 November 2026.*

## In brief
*Le Maître ignorant* (1987) by the French philosopher Jacques Rancière tells of Joseph Jacotot, a French teacher who in 1818 taught at the University of Leuven. He knew no Dutch, and his students knew no French. Even so, they learned French by reading a bilingual edition of the novel *Télémaque* and writing about it. Jacotot drew a bold conclusion: it is not necessary to explain in order to teach, and everyone has an equal capacity to understand. Rancière uses the story to ask what a teacher actually does.

## About the book
The book is divided into five "lessons". Rancière retells Jacotot's experiment and his "universal teaching". The main idea is that the ordinary explaining teacher rests on an assumption: the pupil cannot understand without help. The explanation thus creates the distance it claims to close. Jacotot turns this around. The pupil can learn without explanation, so long as he has something to work with and the will to work. The teacher does not need to know more. His task is to demand attention and to ask: what do you see, what do you think, what do you make of it? Rancière calls this "intellectual emancipation". The book also discusses why the idea was received badly by institutions and reformers. The English translation has an introduction by Kristin Ross, which places the book in a French debate on schooling after 1968.

## From the bookshelf to daily life
This is our own reading, not Rancière's.

**Programming.**
- Programmers learn largely without a teacher: they read documentation, source code and error messages. Getting stuck and getting out of it yourself is a substantial part of the craft. Rancière would say this is not a shortcoming of the training but the learning itself.
- A senior who always solves the problem for a beginner teaches him that he needs someone to solve it. Asking "what have you tried, and what does the log show?" can help more than a finished answer.

**Seafaring.**
- Much is learned on watch on board, not in the classroom. A chief officer who lets the cadet say what he sees on the radar and why, rather than explaining first, does something like Jacotot.
- We must be careful here. In safety-critical tasks, such as following the collision regulations, basic knowledge has to be in place before standing a watch alone. Rancière gives no ground for dropping the Rules or procedures; he argues about how people learn, not about whether the requirements apply.

## Objections
- The Leuven story is one told episode, not a study. The students were adults and motivated, and had a bilingual text to lean on. It is unclear whether the method works for everyone.
- Research on teaching has often found that novices benefit greatly from clear explanation and guidance. Rancière rejects this on principle, which is a choice rather than an empirical result.
- "Equality of intelligences" is for him a starting point one acts from, not a finding. Critics say that makes the theory hard to test.
- The book is written in a dense, literary style that many find demanding.
