## In brief
The *Enchiridion* ("handbook") is a selection of short pieces of advice from the Stoic teacher Epictetus. He did not write it himself: his pupil Arrian collected his teaching. Epictetus was himself born a slave. The idea that carries the whole book stands at the very start: some things are in our power, such as our opinions, aims and desires, and others are not, such as body, property, reputation and office. Calm and freedom come from spending our energy on the first and letting go of the second.

## About the book
The book has 51 short chapters in this edition. Some are direct thought exercises. Epictetus says we should remind ourselves what kind of thing we are fond of: if it is a cup, we know it can break. Others concern our attitude to events. The most quoted is chapter 5, which says we are disturbed "not by things, but by the views" we take of them, that is, by our judgements. He also compares life to a play: we did not choose the part, but our business is to act it well (chapter 17). Chapter 7 uses a ship: on a voyage with the ship at anchor you may go ashore and pick up shellfish, but your thoughts must stay with the ship so you are ready the moment the captain calls.

## From the bookshelf to daily life
This is our own reading, not Epictetus'.

**Programming.**
- The line between what you control and what you do not is useful in development work: your code, your tests and how you read error messages are in your power; the customer's shifting wishes, the libraries you depend on and what a manager thinks of the plan are not.
- Epictetus says the uninstructed person blames others, the beginner blames himself, and the person fully taught blames no one. That resembles a blameless post-incident review: ask what happened and how the system can get better, rather than who is to answer for it.

**Seafaring.**
- An officer cannot decide the weather, the visibility or what another vessel does. The watch, the look-out (Rule 5), safe speed and how early and clearly one manoeuvres are in the officer's power. Putting the energy there is good seamanship.
- Stoicism can be misread as an excuse to do little. The collision regulations say the opposite: Rule 2 (a) exonerates nobody from the consequences of neglecting any precaution required by the ordinary practice of seamen. What is in your power, you must actually do.
- Marcus Aurelius, whom we review separately, makes a similar point with a pilot: he should not be astonished that the wind is contrary.

## Objections
- The advice not to attach too strongly to others (chapter 3 on child and spouse, for instance) can seem cold, and Pascal is among those who criticised Stoic pride early on.
- Epictetus speaks of servants as property and takes slavery as a given frame. That has to be read with today's eyes.
- It can slide into a mild passivity: not everything outside your power is outside your influence. We can vote, organise and build better systems.
- Working through emotions is not the same as overriding them with insight. Modern psychology has more to say about that than Epictetus.
